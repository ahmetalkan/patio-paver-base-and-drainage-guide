Patio Paver Base and Drainage Guide: Why Sub-Base Work Decides Longevity

Interlocking stone or brick pavers look straightforward on the surface, yet virtually every sunken, rutted, or weed-choked patio fails below ground—from thin uncompacted gravel, missed slope away from house siding, or weak edging that lets outer stones creep sideways.

The Concrete Masonry & Hardscapes Association (CMHA, formerly ICPI/NCMA) establishes residential installation standards for interlocking concrete pavement systems, emphasizing compacted aggregate base lifts, thin uniform bedding sand, and rigid perimeter restraint. Where stormwater runoff is a concern, the U.S. EPA's permeable pavement guidance explains how open-graded stone bases allow rain to infiltrate through paver joints instead of flooding neighboring lots.

Pre-excavation checklist

Installation breakdowns and material cost comparisons are available on Paver Lab and its paver patio installation guide.

  1. Call 811 before digging: Shallow cable, gas, irrigation, and landscape wiring often sit right inside typical patio excavation depth.
  2. Set finish elevation below house siding and door thresholds: Slope finished pavers away from structures (commonly ~1/8 to 1/4 inch per foot) so water never drains toward basement walls.
  3. Remove all sod and organic topsoil: Organic roots decompose over time and leave soft depressions under aggregate bases.
  4. Verify paver thickness for traffic type: Pedestrian walkways and patios typically use ~60 mm units; vehicular driveways require thicker heavy-duty profiles and deeper aggregate bases.
Do not use fine play sand or mason sand for bedding or jointing—rounded fine grains shift easily and wash out. Angular washed concrete sand (ASTM C33) locks firmly under vibration.
